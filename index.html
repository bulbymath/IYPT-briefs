IYPT 2027 — Full Problem Briefs

All 17 problems. Verbatim statement, sub-score breakdown, the findings you should expect, and every attack an opponent can land.

D Difficulty = apparatus · reproducibility · measurement A Attack surface = confound count · severity · shield penetration T Theory = closed-form · literature · falsifiable signature V Visual = impact · reliability in a fight · narrative
Pick value = (T + V) − (D + A)

The rule that predicts this entire ranking. Attack surface is set by what the subject is made of.

Problems whose subject is geometry — a grating pitch, two string lengths, an aperture shape — generate their prediction from a ruler measurement. There is no room to argue, and the theory shields you.

Problems whose subject is an ill-defined material — chalk, cornflour, ice, a friction contact, a book spine — hand your opponent a dozen uncontrolled variables for free, and have almost no theory to hide behind. All five Tier-D problems are materials problems.

Y-shaped pendulum

+12
5. Y-shaped pendulum. Construct a Bowditch pendulum (a pendulum whose strings form a “Y” shape). Investigate how the Lissajous patterns generated by the bob depend on the relevant parameters.
MetricSub-scores
D Difficultyapparatus 2reproducibility 2measurement 43
A Attackconfounds 3severity 3penetration 33
T Theoryclosed-form 10literature 8falsifiable 109
V Visualimpact 10reliability 9narrative 89

Likely findings

  • Junction J sits on the circle where two spheres of radius L₁ about A and B intersect, so h = √(L₁² − d²/4). Two normal modes fall out: ω₁ = √(g/(h+L₂)) in J's plane, ω₂ = √(g/L₂) perpendicular to it.
  • Frequency ratio ω₂/ω₁ = √((h+L₂)/L₂) — fully set by geometry. With L₁ = 0.50 m, d = 0.30 m, L₂ = 0.30 m: h = 0.477 m, ratio = 1.61 (a clean 8:5 figure), T₂ = 1.10 s, T₁ = 1.77 s. Tunable across roughly 1.1 to 3 by varying d and L₂.
  • Precession. Real period is T₀(1 + θ₀²/16 + …). At 30° amplitude that is a 1.7% period shift, and because the two modes decay at different rates the ratio drifts and the figure rotates slowly — precession period of order tens of seconds. This is your depth slide.
  • Amplitude decays with Q ≈ 100–1000, so the show lasts 1–3 minutes. Long enough to film, short enough to run live.

Attacks

  1. "You showed pretty photographs. You never predicted the pattern from the geometry before you ran it." If there is no a-priori prediction, you have done nothing.
  2. "Your string has mass." A non-zero string mass shifts both frequencies, and by different amounts, so your ratio is systematically off.
  3. "Your supports flex." Any compliance in the clamps changes the effective length, and you never measured it.
  4. "Finite amplitude." The small-angle formula is not valid at the amplitudes you actually ran; you have an uncorrected systematic error.
  5. "Your two modes are coupled by friction at the knot." The junction is not a frictionless constraint; it bleeds energy between modes and blurs both frequencies.
  6. "Your bob is not a point mass." It has rotational inertia and can spin about the string — a third degree of freedom you ignored.
  7. "Your motion is three-dimensional, not planar." At large amplitude the two "modes" are not clean, so a 2D Lissajous description is not actually valid.
  8. "Air drag acts on the string as well as the bob." Your damping model only accounted for one of them.
Worst attack: #1. Everything else is a percent-level correction; #1 invalidates the whole presentation.
Countermeasures
  • Pre-register. Write down the predicted ω₂/ω₁ for five geometries before building, then tabulate predicted vs measured. Unarguable, and takes an afternoon.
  • Heavy bob (≥200 g) on light braided fishing line — makes string mass negligible; state the ratio.
  • Measure the amplitude decay and fit the anharmonic correction, then show the residual — converts attack #4 into a second result.
  • Rigid supports bolted to a wall, not clamped to a bench; verify by pushing on them.

Photographic bokeh

+8
9. Photographic bokeh. Bokeh is an effect observed in photographs that show objects at different distances from the camera and have both sharp and out-of-focus areas. If a slit is placed in front of the lens of the camera, the image may have unusual anisotropic distortions in out-of-focus areas, while in-focus areas remain undistorted and sharp. Investigate how the properties and sharpness of resulting images depend on relevant parameters.
MetricSub-scores
D Difficultyapparatus 4reproducibility 3measurement 44
A Attackconfounds 5severity 4penetration 34
T Theoryclosed-form 8literature 8falsifiable 98
V Visualimpact 9reliability 8narrative 88

Likely findings

  • Headline result: the blur kernel IS the aperture shape, scaled by defocus. Defocus blur width c ≈ A·|S₂−S₁|/S₂ with A = f/N.
  • For a slit of width w and length L: blur streak length ≈ (L/f)·defocus, width ≈ (w/f)·defocus, so the anisotropy ratio of the blur equals the slit aspect ratio L/w — independent of defocus. That is the crisp prediction to build the whole talk on.
  • In MTF terms: the OTF is the pupil's autocorrelation, so a slit pupil gives a triangular OTF across the slit and a broader one along it. The ratio of the two MTF null frequencies = L/w, independent of defocus. Measurable with a slanted-edge target.
  • In-focus regions stay sharp because the in-focus PSF width is 2.44λN (Airy/sinc), set by diffraction, not by the aperture silhouette — so slit length does not degrade in-focus sharpness, only slit width does, and only in one axis.
  • Expect measurable anisotropy for L/w ≥ ~3; below that it washes out.

Attacks

  1. "How do you know that anisotropy is your slit and not astigmatism in your lens?" Real lenses have field-dependent astigmatism, coma and curvature.
  2. "Your camera applied sharpening." In-camera processing, demosaicing and JPEG compression all alter the PSF; your kernel is partly an artefact of your sensor pipeline.
  3. "You used a white LED." Chromatic aberration means your blur is wavelength-dependent, so your "single" PSF is actually an average over colour.
  4. "Adding the slit changed your exposure." You changed the aperture area, so you conflated a shape effect with a brightness effect — and noise scales with brightness, so your sharpness metric changed for two reasons at once.
  5. "You are in the diffraction-limited regime." At small effective apertures the geometric blur model fails outright.
  6. "Your slit has finite thickness and its edges diffract." You modelled a 2D aperture but built a 3D object.
  7. "Vignetting." Off-axis your effective pupil is clipped by the barrel, so the blur shape changes across the frame and you averaged over it.
  8. "You never measured the PSF directly." Everything you claim is inferred from scene photographs rather than from a point source.
Worst attack: #1, followed closely by #2. Both strike at whether the effect you measured is the one you think it is.
Countermeasures
  • Rotate the slit 0° / 45° / 90° and show the anisotropy rotates with it. Nothing else in the system rotates, so this cleanly separates slit from lens.
  • Shoot with and without the slit at the identical field position — same pixels, same aberration.
  • Shoot RAW, all processing off, manual focus, fixed ISO — and say so explicitly.
  • Narrowband source (green LED or colour filter) to kill chromatic aberration.
  • Normalise exposure across conditions; shoot a dark frame for noise subtraction.
  • Measure the PSF twice: directly from a point source and from a slanted edge. Two independent routes to the same number.

Disc spectrometer

+6
2. Disc spectrometer. Data tracks on optical media (e.g. CDs / Blu-ray discs) behave as high-quality reflection-diffraction gratings. When illuminated, these discs can disperse light into its spectral components. Construct a spectrometer using a digital optical disc. Optimise the design to maximise the resolving power and minimise optical aberrations.
MetricSub-scores
D Difficultyapparatus 7reproducibility 4measurement 76
A Attackconfounds 4severity 4penetration 44
T Theoryclosed-form 10literature 9falsifiable 99
V Visualimpact 7reliability 7narrative 77

Likely findings

  • Track pitch: CD 1.6 µm, DVD 0.74 µm, Blu-ray 0.32 µm. Grating equation mλ = d(sinθᵢ + sinθₘ); resolving power R = λ/Δλ = mN.
  • First-order deviation at λ = 550 nm, normal incidence: CD sinθ = 0.34 → 20°; DVD → 48°; Blu-ray has no first order at all (sinθ = 1.72 > 1) — you must go to oblique incidence. A genuine, non-obvious finding.
  • The tension that IS the problem: R grows with illuminated width W (N = W/d), but the tracks are circular, so the sagitta over width W is W²/(8r). At r = 40 mm, W = 10 mm the sag is 0.31 mm ≈ 195 groove spacings — comparable to the resolution you are trying to buy. So R peaks at an optimum W, and that is what "optimise" actually means here.
  • Expect a measured R of a few hundred to ~2000, well below the ideal mN, limited by slit width and curvature aberration.
  • Secondary findings: using the outer radius (larger r) reduces curvature and improves R; higher orders buy R but cost intensity and suffer order overlap.

Attacks

  1. "You are slit-limited, not grating-limited." Your resolution is set by your slit width, so nothing you say about the grating is demonstrated.
  2. "You ignored the polycarbonate." The beam refracts at the disc's protective layer before reaching the tracks; treating it as a bare grating is a systematic error in d.
  3. "You assumed the track pitch from the spec sheet." Manufacturing tolerance is real and you never measured it.
  4. "'Minimise aberrations' — you never defined a metric." Without a figure of merit (line FWHM, Strehl ratio) the optimisation is unfalsifiable.
  5. "Order overlap." m = 2 at 400 nm lands on m = 1 at 800 nm; you did not sort orders, so your spectrum is contaminated.
  6. "Your detector pixel pitch is your resolution limit." You are undersampling.
  7. "Your collimation is imperfect." A divergent incident beam broadens every line and you never characterised the divergence.
  8. "You only tested one radius on the disc." Your whole argument is about track curvature, so you needed a radius scan — one data point cannot support an optimisation claim.
Worst attack: #1. It collapses "optimise the resolving power" into "you measured your slit."
Countermeasures
  • Resolve the sodium D doublet (589.0 / 589.6 nm, Δλ = 0.6 nm ⇒ R ≈ 1000). Demonstrated resolution ends the argument.
  • Plot R vs slit width for three widths and show saturation — proves grating-limited.
  • Measure d yourself by diffracting a known laser (532 or 632.8 nm) and back out the pitch.
  • Define the merit function up front (line FWHM in nm) and state it in the intro.
  • Scan radius and report R(r) — turns #8 into your central figure.

Singing capacitor

+5
4. Singing capacitor. Certain types of ceramic capacitors emit an audible sound when an alternating voltage is applied across them. Investigate the characteristics of the sound produced and how they depend on relevant parameters, such as voltage, frequency, and capacitor properties.
MetricSub-scores
D Difficultyapparatus 5reproducibility 4measurement 44
A Attackconfounds 5severity 4penetration 34
T Theoryclosed-form 8literature 8falsifiable 98
V Visualimpact 4reliability 6narrative 65

Likely findings

  • Headline: drive at f, hear 2f. Electrostriction gives strain ∝ E², and cos²ωt contains a 2ω term. Crisp, falsifiable, memorable.
  • Displacement ∝ V²; sound pressure ∝ f·V², with strong amplification at the mechanical resonances of the capacitor-plus-PCB system — typically 1–10 kHz, which is exactly why you can hear it.
  • Dielectric-class control: X7R / Y5V (ferroelectric BaTiO₃) sing loudly; C0G / NP0 (paraelectric) are essentially silent. Expect a 30–40 dB difference.
  • Package size: 1210 and 1812 are far louder than 0402 and 0603.
  • DC bias lowers Class II permittivity, so SPL falls monotonically with added DC bias — a second clean parameter.
  • MLCC acoustic noise is a real industry reliability problem (Murata, TDK and KEMET all publish on it), so there is literature to cite.

Attacks

  1. "Your microphone was never calibrated." An uncalibrated or phone mic has unknown frequency response, so your SPL spectrum is meaningless.
  2. "You measured the room, not the capacitor." Reflections and standing waves make SPL position-dependent; move the mic and you get a different answer.
  3. "The sound comes from the PCB, not the capacitor." The board is the soundboard — you never isolated the source.
  4. "You assumed the voltage across the part." ESR and ESL matter at these frequencies; the actual terminal voltage is not your signal-generator setting.
  5. "You did not rule out alternative mechanisms." Magnetostriction in the leads, Lorentz force, loose solder joints — you picked electrostriction without eliminating rivals.
  6. "Amplitude ∝ V² is only approximate." DC-bias-dependent permittivity and mechanical nonlinearity mean your scaling law has unmodelled corrections.
  7. "You changed the value and the package at the same time." Two variables moved, one conclusion drawn.
  8. "Class II dielectrics age logarithmically and are temperature-dependent." Your capacitor changed during the session.
Worst attack: #1. If your SPL axis is uncalibrated, every quantitative claim collapses. Lead with the frequency-domain result instead.
Countermeasures
  • Lead with 2f and the C0G vs X7R control. Both are mechanism-level and frequency-domain, so they survive a calibration argument entirely.
  • Calibrate against a reference mic, or at minimum report relative SPL and say so plainly.
  • Vary board size, or suspend the capacitor on fine wires, to show the board is an amplifier and not the source.
  • Measure the true terminal voltage with a scope probe at the part.
  • Fix the package and vary only the dielectric, then fix the dielectric and vary only the package.

Seeing sound

+4
6. Seeing sound. A thin rubber membrane is stretched over a circular frame and driven into vibration by a loudspeaker. A small mirror is attached to the membrane, and a laser beam is reflected off it onto a distant screen. Under certain conditions, the laser spot traces out complex, steady, or evolving geometric patterns. Investigate the relationship between the driving parameters, the membrane’s properties, and the resulting light patterns.
MetricSub-scores
D Difficultyapparatus 6reproducibility 6measurement 66
A Attackconfounds 6severity 6penetration 56
T Theoryclosed-form 8literature 6falsifiable 77
V Visualimpact 10reliability 8narrative 99

Likely findings

  • Circular membrane modes: f_nm = (α_nm / 2πa)·√(T/σ), α_nm the Bessel zeros. For a = 5 cm, σ ≈ 0.1 kg/m², T ≈ 10 N/m the fundamental is ≈ 77 Hz.
  • Mode frequency ratios are fixed: α_nm/α₀₁ = 1 : 1.59 : 2.14 : 2.30 : 2.65 : 2.92 : 3.16 : 3.50 : 3.60. Measuring these ratios is a strong, specific test of the membrane model — and their deviation is your measure of non-ideality.
  • Subtlety worth the talk: the mirror reports the local slope, not the displacement, so the traced figure is the gradient field of the mode — related to a Chladni figure but not the same thing. Explaining why is a differentiator.
  • Off resonance or at high drive: mode mixing, beating, period-doubling and evolving/chaotic patterns — that is the "evolving" regime the problem names.
  • Rubber is viscoelastic, so Q is low (order 10–50) and modes are broad and overlap; expect difficulty separating close modes.

Attacks

  1. "That mirror is a significant fraction of your membrane's mass." You measured the mirror-loaded membrane, not the membrane.
  2. "Rubber creeps." Tension drifts over minutes, so your mode frequencies drift and your repeat measurements are at different tensions.
  3. "Acoustic or mechanical coupling?" If the frame is shaking, this is "driven by the bench," not by a loudspeaker.
  4. "Bessel theory assumes uniform tension and a perfectly clamped circular edge." You have neither — clamping is uneven and tension is never uniform in a hand-stretched sheet.
  5. "Your laser spot has finite size." Your resolution limit is the beam, and the small deflections sit inside it.
  6. "Air currents." The room is not still, and the speaker is moving air across your beam.
  7. "You never measured T or σ." Your whole prediction depends on two numbers you estimated rather than measured.
  8. "The mirror rocks on its glue joint." You never verified it is rigidly coupled to the membrane.
Worst attack: #1. It is quantitative, easy to raise, and hard to wave away without having done the work.
Countermeasures
  • Measure f₀₁ for three mirror masses and extrapolate to zero mass. Converts the attack into a result and gives you the unloaded frequency.
  • Measure T directly (a small force gauge on the rim, or a calibrated deflection) and σ by weighing a known area.
  • Isolate the speaker mechanically from the frame (separate tables) to prove the coupling is acoustic; then test with the speaker touching to show the difference.
  • Re-tension or replace the membrane on a fixed schedule and log time-since-stretching.

Vortex pendulum

+3
13. Vortex pendulum. A pendulum with a vertically oriented cylindrical bob is suspended above a channel of flowing water. When the bob is partially submerged, the flow may cause the pendulum to oscillate in a direction perpendicular to the flow. Explain this phenomenon and determine how it depends on relevant parameters.
MetricSub-scores
D Difficultyapparatus 9reproducibility 6measurement 88
A Attackconfounds 5severity 5penetration 45
T Theoryclosed-form 9literature 9falsifiable 99
V Visualimpact 7reliability 6narrative 87

Likely findings

  • This is vortex-induced vibration. Shedding frequency f_s = St·U/D with St ≈ 0.2 for 300 < Re < 2×10⁵.
  • Numbers: D = 3 cm, U = 0.2 m/s ⇒ f_s = 1.33 Hz. Matching f_n = (1/2π)√(g/L) = 1.33 Hz needs L ≈ 14 cm. Entirely buildable.
  • Lock-in: amplitude peaks when f_s/f_n ≈ 1, over a band roughly 0.8–1.3. Peak amplitude of order 0.5–1.5 D (1.5–4.5 cm).
  • Hysteresis: the lock-in band differs when you sweep U upward versus downward. A classic VIV signature and an excellent "we went further" result.
  • Submersion depth matters — only the wetted length sheds vortices, so expect an optimum submersion.
  • Theory armor is excellent: Strouhal, lock-in, wake-oscillator models (Facchinetti et al.). The only reason this is not Tier A is the flume. With a channel available, D drops to ~4 and this becomes #2.

Attacks

  1. "Your channel is barely wider than your bob — you measured the walls." Blockage changes the shedding entirely.
  2. "Free surface." Partially submerged means Froude number matters too; you have two dimensionless groups and varied only one.
  3. "Your flow is not uniform and you do not know its turbulence intensity." A DIY channel has a developing, sheared profile.
  4. "How did you measure U?" A propeller meter in a 10 cm channel perturbs the flow it measures.
  5. "Bottom end effects." The bob's lower face is near the floor; the tip vortex and gap flow are unmodelled.
  6. "Your Re is outside the range where St ≈ 0.2." In a small channel you may be at Re of a few hundred, where Strouhal is strongly Re-dependent.
  7. "Meniscus and contact-angle effects at the waterline." The wetting condition at the bob is uncontrolled.
  8. "Is it VIV, or is the pendulum just responding to surface waves you generated?" You never separated the two excitations.
Worst attack: #1. Blockage is a genuine first-order correction in any lab-scale channel and cannot be removed by technique.
Countermeasures
  • Run two bob diameters and show both collapse onto St ≈ 0.2 when properly scaled — blockage then becomes a stated correction rather than a refutation.
  • Measure U with dye PIV (non-intrusive) or upstream of the bob, and report the profile, not a single number.
  • Add a surface-piercing wave damper upstream and downstream to isolate VIV from wave forcing — directly answers #8.
  • Report Re and the blockage ratio explicitly on every plot.

Magnetic carousel

+2
16. Magnetic carousel. Several neodymium magnets are attached to a horizontal disc, with adjacent magnets oriented in opposite directions. A fixed non-magnetic plate is placed horizontally above the disc. When the disc rotates about its vertical axis and a steel ball is placed on the plate, the ball may move either in the same direction as, or opposite to, the rotation of the disc. Investigate this phenomenon and determine how the motion depends on relevant parameters.
MetricSub-scores
D Difficultyapparatus 5reproducibility 5measurement 55
A Attackconfounds 7severity 6penetration 56
T Theoryclosed-form 4literature 4falsifiable 75
V Visualimpact 9reliability 8narrative 78

Likely findings

  • Alternating polarity means the field pattern repeats every two magnets, so the travelling potential has period 2× the magnet spacing.
  • Two regimes, and the boundary is the result: at low disc speed the ball locks into a potential minimum and co-rotates (synchronous-motor-like); above a critical speed it cannot keep up, slips, and the kicks average to a net push against the rotation (induction-motor slip). A ball moving backwards while the disc moves forwards is one of the best visual hooks on the list.
  • The critical speed ω_c is set by F_mag,max ≈ µ_s·mg. Since the dipole force falls steeply with gap (~1/r⁴), ω_c drops sharply as plate thickness increases. Expect a clean ω_c(gap) curve.
  • Intermediate speeds: intermittent motion, the ball jumping between minima, possibly stick-slip.
  • v_ball vs ω_disc should be piecewise with a clear sign change — that plot is the talk.

Attacks

  1. "That ball has been sitting next to neodymium magnets all afternoon. It is magnetised." Remanent magnetisation with history dependence — its behaviour depends on what you did to it earlier.
  2. "Your friction is uncontrolled, and friction is half the physics." Plate material, dust, humidity, rolling vs sliding all move the answer.
  3. "Your plate is not level." Gravity biases the ball downhill and you never checked with a spirit level.
  4. "Plate thickness sets the gap, and the dipole force goes as ~1/r⁴." A 0.2 mm variation in your plate dominates your result.
  5. "Your magnets are not identical." Neo magnets carry ±5–10% strength tolerance plus magnetisation-direction tolerance.
  6. "Your motor speed droops under load." You set a voltage, not a speed.
  7. "The ball rolls rather than slides." Your friction model assumed sliding.
  8. "Air currents from the spinning disc." You have a fan under the plate.
Worst attack: #1 — genuinely nasty because it is true and almost nobody controls for it.
Countermeasures
  • Fresh ball every trial, or demagnetise between runs, and state which you did.
  • Measure and report the gap (plate thickness) as a stated parameter; run a gap sweep.
  • Level the plate with a spirit level and re-check after every run.
  • Closed-loop motor speed, or at minimum a tachometer logged continuously.
  • Enclose the disc, or show that shielding it from airflow changes nothing.

Invent yourself: Pinhole sunglasses

−1
1. Invent yourself: Pinhole sunglasses. Investigate the optimal arrangement of pinholes in an opaque film to produce sunglasses that reduce light intensity and help correct short-sightedness.
MetricSub-scores
D Difficultyapparatus 6reproducibility 5measurement 76
A Attackconfounds 8severity 6penetration 77
T Theoryclosed-form 7literature 5falsifiable 56
V Visualimpact 6reliability 6narrative 66

Likely findings

  • Two independent effects: light reduction = open-area fraction (≈ (π/4)(d/p)² on a square lattice), and the pinhole effect = increased depth of field.
  • Optimal diameter from the geometric-blur vs diffraction trade-off: d_opt ≈ 1.9√(λf). At λ = 550 nm and f = 17 mm (the eye), d_opt ≈ 0.18 mm.
  • The finding nobody expects: a pinhole cannot give you sharp vision. Diffraction limits it to θ ≈ 1.22λ/d ≈ 3.7 mrad ≈ 12 arcmin, versus 1 arcmin for a normal eye. Best-case pinhole acuity is around 20/200 on a Snellen chart. Pinhole glasses buy depth of field — everything equally, moderately blurry — not sharpness. A severe myope improves a lot; a mild one may get worse.
  • The insight that makes this problem worth taking: a regular pinhole array is a 2D diffraction grating, so white light produces coloured diffraction orders. A periodic arrangement is arguably the wrong answer, and a randomised arrangement may beat it. That is a real design conclusion.
  • Expect an optimum spacing-to-diameter ratio p/d ≈ 2–5: denser and diffraction/overlap degrades the image; sparser and you get multiple overlapping images.

Attacks

  1. "Your eye model is not an eye." A camera plus one lens has no curved retina, no accommodation, no chromatic aberration, and no pupil that reacts to light.
  2. "Your regular array is a diffraction grating." You introduced colour fringing and higher orders and never mentioned it.
  3. "Your optimum is prescription-dependent." −2 D and −6 D give different answers, so you have no general result.
  4. "You darkened the image, so the pupil dilated — undoing the pinhole effect." On a real eye the two effects fight each other.
  5. "Acuity depends on luminance." You cut the light, so you moved into mesopic vision where acuity falls for reasons unrelated to optics.
  6. "Your holes have burrs and are not identical." Burrs scatter light; hand-drilled holes vary in diameter.
  7. "d_opt is wavelength-dependent and you tested in white light." Your optimum is smeared across the visible spectrum.
  8. "You reduced the field of view." You improved one metric and ignored another the problem implicitly cares about.
Worst attack: #1 combined with #3 — "you optimised one prescription on a camera that is not an eye, so what is the general result?"
Countermeasures
  • Sweep the defocus parameter and report acuity vs defocus rather than a single optimum — that gives you a general result.
  • Compare periodic vs randomised arrays explicitly and show the diffraction orders in the periodic case. Turns #2 into your result.
  • Hold retinal illuminance constant across conditions (compensate with ND filters) to kill #4 and #5.
  • Use precision apertures; microscope-image the holes and report the diameter distribution.

Snail ball

−1
3. Snail ball. A hollow sphere contains a heavy ball and some viscous fluid. The rolling motion of such a sphere down an inclined plane is uneven and surprisingly slow. Explain and investigate this effect.
MetricSub-scores
D Difficultyapparatus 6reproducibility 6measurement 66
A Attackconfounds 7severity 7penetration 77
T Theoryclosed-form 4literature 5falsifiable 55
V Visualimpact 7reliability 7narrative 77

Likely findings

  • Mechanism: the inner heavy ball lags the shell. Gravity torque on it is transmitted through viscous shear in the fluid, which dissipates energy continuously — hence slow.
  • Uneven because the inner ball periodically repositions relative to the shell; centre of mass and effective moment of inertia change through the cycle, so acceleration is not constant. Expect speed fluctuations of 20–50% with a period of order 1–3 s.
  • Best quantitative result available: measure the effective moment of inertia and show it is less than the rigid-body value — direct evidence that the inner ball does not co-rotate.
  • Clean scaling to test: mean speed ∝ 1/µ. Vary viscosity (different oils, or controlled temperature) and you should get a clean inverse relation. This is the one falsifiable prediction here.
  • Contrast with the solid-sphere baseline: a rolling solid sphere reaches v² = (10/7)gh. The snail ball sits far below that and goes roughly linear in sinθ rather than as √h, because it is viscous-limited rather than inertia-limited.

Attacks

  1. "Viscosity changes several percent per degree. Did you log the fluid temperature?" Glycerol and silicone oil are strongly temperature-dependent; room drift alone ruins reproducibility.
  2. "Did it actually roll without slipping?" You never verified — mark the shell and check.
  3. "There is a trapped air bubble." It completely changes the dissipation and you cannot see it.
  4. "Your fill fraction sets the gap, and the gap sets the dissipation." Unreported, and it is the dominant geometric parameter.
  5. "You have no clean observable for 'uneven'." The motion is quasi-periodic — what is your dependent variable, and what is its error bar?
  6. "Rolling resistance of the shell itself is a background you never subtracted." Hysteresis loss in the shell dissipates energy too.
  7. "Surface roughness of the inner ball and the shell's inner wall." Both set the shear and neither is specified.
  8. "How many trials?" With a stochastic element, three runs cannot support a claim about periodicity.
Worst attack: #1. Any viscous problem lives or dies on temperature logging, and it is the first thing a good opponent asks.
Countermeasures
  • Log fluid temperature on every run; better, use a water bath to hold it constant.
  • Run a viscosity sweep (three or four oils) and show v̄ ∝ 1/µ — that scaling is your shield.
  • Degass the fluid and fill under vacuum, or at minimum report the fill fraction and photograph for bubbles.
  • Paint a stripe on the shell and verify rolling without slipping from the video.
  • Define the observable precisely: mean speed, plus amplitude and period of the speed oscillation via FFT.

Sinking funnel

−2
7. Sinking funnel. When a long-stemmed and sufficiently heavy funnel is released vertically into water with the stem pointing downward, it undergoes a series of damped vertical oscillations while gradually sinking. Investigate the motion and stability of the sinking funnel and the conditions under which the funnel tips over.
MetricSub-scores
D Difficultyapparatus 3reproducibility 5measurement 44
A Attackconfounds 8severity 7penetration 67
T Theoryclosed-form 4literature 3falsifiable 54
V Visualimpact 5reliability 5narrative 55

Likely findings

  • Mechanism: air is trapped in the cone while water enters the open stem. The oscillation is a relaxation cycle — water ingresses, air escapes, the pocket compresses and re-expands, buoyancy changes, repeat.
  • The trapped pocket is a gas spring: stiffness k ≈ γP₀A²/V₀, giving f = (1/2π)√(k/m_eff) of order 1–5 Hz.
  • The best finding available: the frequency chirps upward as it sinks. As depth z rises, P = P₀ + ρgz shrinks V, so k grows and f rises. A measured chirp is a specific, non-obvious, quantitative signature.
  • Damping from viscous drag on the stem plus the inertance of the water column oscillating in the stem.
  • Tipping: expect a threshold tied to oscillation amplitude relative to the metacentric height, or to the air pocket becoming asymmetric. Needs many trials per condition to locate statistically.

Attacks

  1. "Your spring constant changes with depth, so this is not a damped harmonic oscillator." What exactly did you fit, and over what depth range?
  2. "Two-phase flow in the stem is chaotic." Water-in/air-out depends on wetting and surface tension; it is not deterministic.
  3. "Your release imparted rotation or tilt." Initial conditions dominate a bifurcation and your release was by hand.
  4. "Your funnel geometry has five parameters and you varied one." Cone angle, stem length, stem bore, mass, mass distribution.
  5. "Tank wall effects." Your tank is not much larger than the funnel.
  6. "Surface tension." Water purity and trace surfactant change the air pocket behaviour entirely; tap water varies day to day.
  7. "How did you define 'tips over'?" A threshold needs an operational definition and enough trials to locate it — you have neither.
  8. "Air dissolves in water over time." Long runs lose pocket volume by a mechanism you ignored.
Worst attack: #1. It is mathematically correct and it invalidates the standard analysis everyone reaches for first.
Countermeasures
  • Fit only locally — one or two cycles at a time — and plot f versus depth. The chirp then becomes your result rather than your error.
  • Build a mechanical release (electromagnet or clamp) and state zero initial velocity.
  • Use deionised water, log temperature, and keep the water source consistent.
  • Define tipping operationally (e.g. tilt exceeds 45°) and run ≥30 trials per condition to build a tipping probability curve.

Falling book cover

−2
17. Falling book cover. A book is placed horizontally, and the partially open cover is allowed to fall. Investigate the motion of the falling book cover.
MetricSub-scores
D Difficultyapparatus 2reproducibility 5measurement 54
A Attackconfounds 8severity 6penetration 77
T Theoryclosed-form 6literature 4falsifiable 45
V Visualimpact 4reliability 5narrative 34

Likely findings

  • Rigid-body baseline: a uniform plate pivoting about the spine has I = mL²/3 and θ̈ = (3g/2L)cos θ. For L = 0.25 m that is ≈ 59 rad/s² initially, giving a fall time of order 0.3–0.5 s.
  • The honest finding: air drag is small. For a typical cover (0.05 kg, 0.045 m²), gravity torque ≈ 0.061 N·m while peak drag torque ≈ 7×10⁻⁴ N·m — about 1%. So the dominant deviation from rigid-body motion is the spine, not the air. That is a genuinely interesting discriminating result, because almost everyone assumes the opposite.
  • You can invert the motion to extract the spine's torque–angle relation, including its hysteresis loop. That curve is the deliverable.
  • Expect the fall to be slower than rigid-body, with non-monotonic deceleration and usually an audible slap at the end.

Attacks

  1. "Your book had been opened before." The spine is a viscoelastic joint whose properties change with use — you never controlled the opening history.
  2. "Humidity." Paper and board are hygroscopic; stiffness and mass both change with RH. In Hong Kong this is a serious, unavoidable problem.
  3. "Your cover is not a rigid plate." It flexes and twists, so a single-degree-of-freedom rigid model is wrong.
  4. "You cannot vary air drag independently." So you cannot actually isolate the drag term you claim to have measured.
  5. "Book-to-book variability." n = 1 book means your result is a property of that object, not of a phenomenon.
  6. "The pages underneath." Trapped air between pages, and friction against the cover, both matter and both vary with page count.
  7. "What was the initial angle, and does the spine have a preferred resting angle?" You never characterised the initial condition.
  8. "Why should anyone care?" Narrative is nearly absent here.
Worst attack: #1 plus #5 together — "you characterised one specific book's spine, not a phenomenon."
Countermeasures
  • Condition every book identically (e.g. open–close 50 times on a jig) and report RH on every run.
  • Test several books spanning hardcover / paperback / new / broken-in, and present the spine torque curves as a family.
  • Add mass to the cover (taped weights) to separate inertial from dissipative terms.
  • Explicitly compare the air-drag prediction against the measured discrepancy and show drag is ~1% — that negative result is the interesting one.

Sound isolation

−4
11. Sound isolation. The sound isolation of a room may become breached if the door is not shut properly, even if the gap is negligibly small. Investigate how the shape and size of a small opening affect the transmission of sound.
MetricSub-scores
D Difficultyapparatus 8reproducibility 6measurement 77
A Attackconfounds 6severity 6penetration 56
T Theoryclosed-form 7literature 7falsifiable 67
V Visualimpact 2reliability 3narrative 22

Likely findings

  • The aperture is not a passive hole — it is a resonant neck with acoustic mass. Diffraction lets it collect energy from an area larger than its own, so transmission loss drops far more than the area ratio suggests. Classic Gomperts / Wilson–Soroka territory.
  • TL falls roughly as 20·log₁₀ of aperture area — doubling the area costs about 6 dB.
  • Shape matters at fixed area: a slit transmits more than a circle, because the higher perimeter-to-area ratio changes the end correction and the viscous losses.
  • Depth matters and is usually forgotten: increasing panel thickness lengthens the neck and raises TL. Expect a clean monotonic trend.
  • At very narrow gaps, viscous and thermal boundary-layer losses add resistance and TL rises faster than area alone predicts — a second regime worth mapping.
  • The theory is genuinely good. The problem is that you need a real acoustic setup and the payoff is a dB plot nobody remembers.

Attacks

  1. "Prove your box was airtight." Flanking transmission — sound going around the partition — makes the data meaningless if the seals leak.
  2. "Room modes." At low frequency your field is not diffuse, so your TL depends on where you stood.
  3. "You hit the noise floor." At high TL you measured background noise, not transmission.
  4. "Your mic was not calibrated, and near-field vs far-field matters."
  5. "Your panel vibrates and re-radiates." You measured structure-borne transmission, not airborne.
  6. "Edge diffraction at the panel boundary." Your finite panel is not the infinite baffle in the theory.
  7. "You varied area and shape together." Two parameters, one conclusion.
  8. "You never reported the panel thickness." It is first-order and you omitted it.
Worst attack: #1. If you cannot demonstrate the box is otherwise sealed, the entire dataset is void.
Countermeasures
  • Measure the sealed baseline first and show it is 20+ dB below every aperture case. One plot answers #1.
  • Use an impedance tube with the two-microphone transfer-function method if at all possible — it removes the room entirely.
  • Report thickness, area, shape and perimeter for every case in one table.
  • Vary shape at fixed area and depth, then vary depth at fixed shape — one parameter at a time.

Dotted line trick

−4
12. Dotted line trick. Under certain conditions, when a piece of chalk is dragged across a blackboard, the resulting trace is not a continuous line but a periodic sequence of dots, even though the motion is uninterrupted. This can happen with other writing materials and surfaces. Investigate the physical parameters that govern this phenomenon.
MetricSub-scores
D Difficultyapparatus 3reproducibility 5measurement 54
A Attackconfounds 9severity 9penetration 99
T Theoryclosed-form 4literature 4falsifiable 54
V Visualimpact 5reliability 5narrative 55

Likely findings

  • Mechanism is stick-slip: the chalk alternately sticks and slips, depositing material during stick and skipping during slip. Dot spacing s = v / f.
  • Estimate the frequency: chalk as a cantilever beam, k = 3EI/L³. With L = 50 mm, d = 10 mm, E ≈ 5 GPa, m ≈ 5 g you get f ≈ 500 Hz. At v = 0.1 m/s that gives s ≈ 0.2 mm — sub-millimetre dots, which matches what you actually see.
  • The killer experiment, and the only thing that would make this problem worth taking: the same stick-slip produces the chalk's squeal, so f_audio × s = v. Record the squeal, measure the dot spacing, and show they agree. Two independent measurements of one frequency is the strongest defence available here.
  • Expect spacing to increase linearly with speed, and to depend on normal force, chalk angle, hardness and humidity.
  • Competing mechanism to discriminate: chalk is a brittle porous solid and may simply fracture periodically rather than stick-slip. The two predict different scalings.

Attacks

  1. "Your chalk got shorter during the run." Length, contact geometry and contact area all change continuously, so every data point is at a different condition.
  2. "Humidity." Chalk is hygroscopic; friction and fracture both change sharply with RH. You did not log it.
  3. "Chalk composition is not standardised." Not between sticks, and not along one stick.
  4. "The dust from your previous stroke changed the friction for the next one." Your trials are not independent.
  5. "You dragged it by hand." Normal force and speed were both uncontrolled.
  6. "Chalk is a beam — its stiffness matters, and it changes as it shortens." Your frequency model has a time-varying parameter you ignored.
  7. "You have two mechanisms and did not separate them." Stick-slip and periodic fracture predict different scalings; you only tested one.
  8. "The board's surface roughness varies across its width." You moved to a fresh spot between trials.
Worst attack: #1. Unavoidable — the measurement literally destroys the sample — and there is no cheap fix.
Countermeasures
  • Do the audio cross-check (f·s = v). It is the one result that survives the materials swamp, because it is an internal consistency test rather than an absolute measurement.
  • Mechanised carriage with a dead-weight normal load; replace the chalk every N centimetres and report it.
  • Log RH and temperature; ideally run in a controlled-humidity enclosure.
  • Test a non-friable stylus on a rough surface to separate stick-slip from fracture.

Non-Newtonian worms

−5
14. Non-Newtonian worms. A dilatant non-Newtonian fluid (e.g. a suspension of water and cornflour) is vibrated vertically in a reservoir. When certain conditions are met, wormlike structures that rise from the surface may form. Explain and investigate this phenomenon.
MetricSub-scores
D Difficultyapparatus 7reproducibility 8measurement 77
A Attackconfounds 9severity 9penetration 109
T Theoryclosed-form 1literature 2falsifiable 32
V Visualimpact 10reliability 8narrative 99

Likely findings

  • Typical working suspension: roughly 60 wt% cornflour (the usual oobleck). Shear thickening onsets at shear rates of order 1–10 s⁻¹.
  • Worms appear above a critical dimensionless acceleration Γ = Aω²/g, typically Γ ≈ 2–5 — the layer must go into partial free fall each cycle. Shaker frequencies in the 20–100 Hz range.
  • Worm height grows then saturates; growth rate depends on Γ and layer depth. Worms persist for seconds after shaking stops — a memory effect, and the honest headline.
  • The one good control available: a Newtonian oil of matched viscosity does not form worms, showing shear thickening is necessary.
  • Related to the Faraday instability but with history-dependent rheology — the analogy is suggestive, not quantitative.

Attacks

  1. "Show me two identical runs." The suspension settles, dries and remembers its shear history — every run is a different material.
  2. "You did not control the amplitude." A speaker's displacement varies with frequency at constant drive voltage; you need accelerometer feedback, and most teams fudge this.
  3. "Your concentration was never measured by weight." And cornflour varies by brand and source.
  4. "It dried out during the session." Over 40 minutes the water content changes measurably.
  5. "Your container shape and layer depth changed the answer." Both are first-order and often unreported.
  6. "You have no theory." With T = 2 there is no closed form to hide behind — every objection lands directly.
  7. "Temperature." The water phase viscosity and the thickening threshold both move with it.
  8. "Is it even steady?" Far-from-equilibrium pattern formation with no defined steady state — so what is your measured quantity?
Worst attack: #1, amplified by #6. With no theory shield, a reproducibility objection is fatal rather than merely damaging.
Countermeasures
  • Closed-loop amplitude control with an accelerometer — non-negotiable.
  • Fresh suspension per run, mixed to a measured weight fraction, covered between runs, used within a fixed time window.
  • Run the Newtonian control at matched viscosity to show the effect needs shear thickening.
  • Report the persistence time after shaking stops — a robust observable nobody else will have.

Cold drink

−5
15. Cold drink. A can or bottle of drink at room temperature can be cooled in a cooler containing ice. Adding salt and alcohol to the ice can increase the rate of cooling and affect the drink’s final temperature. Investigate how the relevant parameters affect the lowest achievable temperature and the maximum cooling rate.
MetricSub-scores
D Difficultyapparatus 2reproducibility 3measurement 22
A Attackconfounds 10severity 10penetration 1010
T Theoryclosed-form 6literature 5falsifiable 45
V Visualimpact 2reliability 3narrative 22

Likely findings

  • Two different questions are conflated in one prompt, and separating them is the single best thing you can do here. "Lowest achievable temperature" is equilibrium thermodynamics (freezing-point depression); "maximum cooling rate" is heat transfer (h·A·ΔT). They have almost nothing to do with each other.
  • Lowest temperature: the NaCl–H₂O eutectic is −21.1 °C at 23.3 wt% NaCl. A ternary with ethanol can go lower, perhaps −30 °C. In practice an ice:salt mix at roughly 3:1 by mass gives a brine around −15 to −18 °C.
  • Cooling rate: for a 330 mL can, mc ≈ 1381 J/K and A ≈ 0.031 m². Stirred brine gives h ≈ 1000 W/m²K so τ ≈ 45 s; still air at h ≈ 10 gives τ ≈ 74 min. That is a ~100× difference — a big, clean, defensible number.
  • The lowest achievable temperature is bounded by the eutectic and has essentially nothing to do with the drink — which is itself a worthwhile conclusion.

Attacks

  1. "What temperature was your ice when it came out of the freezer?" Ice at −18 °C versus ice resting at 0 °C differ enormously in sensible heat, and it dominates early cooling. The single biggest confound on the entire list.
  2. "Does the ice actually touch the can?" Air gaps are insulating; a brine bath and a dry ice/salt heap are completely different heat-transfer regimes.
  3. "Did you stir?" Convection changes h by a large factor.
  4. "Ice grain size and shape." Crushed, cubed and block ice have wildly different surface areas — hence rates.
  5. "Salt grain size." Dissolution rate sets how fast you approach the eutectic.
  6. "Alcohol does four different things." It depresses the freezing point, changes heat capacity and density, and evaporates. One variable, four consequences.
  7. "Is that a steady state or a transient minimum?" The bath is warming while the drink cools — your "lowest achievable temperature" may be an artefact of timing.
  8. "Are you measuring the drink or the can wall?" The drink stratifies thermally.
  9. "Cooler insulation, lid opening, ambient temperature, meltwater drainage." All uncontrolled, all first-order.
Worst attack: #1. Almost nobody records it, and it can move the headline cooling rate by a factor of two.
Countermeasures
  • Condition the ice to 0 °C in an ice-water bath before every run, or measure and record its temperature. This alone removes the largest confound on the board.
  • Split the investigation explicitly into equilibrium (final temperature) and transport (rate), and say so in the first slide.
  • Standardise contact: fully submerge in brine, or use a defined ice/salt geometry — never a loose heap.
  • Mechanically stir at a fixed rate, or state "unstirred" and keep it constant.
  • Measure the drink at a defined point with gentle agitation, and log the bath temperature alongside.

Air vortex

−6
10. Air vortex. Place a small rod-shaped magnet on the bottom of a vessel filled with liquid and set it into rotation using a magnetic stirrer. Above a certain rotation speed, a thin air channel forms, extending from the liquid surface down to the rotating magnet itself. Investigate and explain this phenomenon.
MetricSub-scores
D Difficultyapparatus 7reproducibility 7measurement 77
A Attackconfounds 9severity 8penetration 109
T Theoryclosed-form 3literature 3falsifiable 43
V Visualimpact 7reliability 7narrative 77

Likely findings

  • The stir bar drives bulk rotation; the surface takes the solid-body paraboloid z(r) = Ω²r²/(2g). The Rankine structure is a forced (solid-body) core inside r_c and a free vortex outside.
  • Critical speed is the headline number: the central depression reaches the bottom when Ω²R²/(2g) = H, so Ω_crit = √(2gH)/R. For H = 5 cm, R = 3 cm that is ≈ 33 rad/s, about 315 rpm — comfortably within a lab stirrer's range.
  • Expect the channel radius to shrink as Ω increases past threshold, and Ω_crit to scale as √H/R — probably the cleanest result available.
  • The core is stabilised by the secondary (Ekman) flow driven by the bottom boundary layer — which is why the bottom matters at all.
  • But it is a free-boundary two-phase problem, so there is no clean scaling law to anchor the whole thing, and that is fatal in a fight.

Attacks

  1. "Your vessel is only a few times wider than your vortex core — you measured your beaker." The vessel-radius-to-core-radius ratio sets everything, and wall effects dominate.
  2. "Surface tension." Thin-channel versus broad-funnel is decided by it, and trace detergent contamination changes it completely.
  3. "Liquid depth and fill level." Both first-order, both often unreported.
  4. "Viscosity, and its temperature dependence." A stirrer's motor heats the plate.
  5. "You changed the stir bar." Bar shape and size change the answer.
  6. "Your stirrer speed is not calibrated and droops under load."
  7. "Dissolved gas and bubbles." Nucleation on the magnet changes the channel formation.
  8. "Refraction at the curved vessel wall." Your measurement of "thin" is itself distorted by the glass.
  9. "No scaling law." With T = 3 you have nothing to deflect any of the above.
Worst attack: #1. Unlike most confounds it is not fixable by better technique — it is a statement that your geometry is wrong.
Countermeasures
  • Run a vessel-diameter series and show Ω_crit ∝ 1/R. If the scaling holds, the wall effect becomes part of your model rather than an objection.
  • Use a rectangular tank and image through flat walls to kill refraction.
  • Tachometer on the stirrer (optical or via the drive magnet), logged continuously.
  • Deionised water, fresh for every run, surfactant-free glassware.

Upward-driven disc

−8
8. Upward-driven disc. A circular disc is suspended from the point where it is sandwiched between two wheels, making it free to oscillate as a pendulum. One of the wheels is driven by a motor and thus exerts an upward driving force on the disc. Investigate the motion of the disc.
MetricSub-scores
D Difficultyapparatus 8reproducibility 9measurement 78
A Attackconfounds 8severity 9penetration 88
T Theoryclosed-form 4literature 4falsifiable 44
V Visualimpact 4reliability 4narrative 44

Likely findings

  • The disc is a pendulum suspended at the contact point; the drive is transmitted by dry friction from the driven wheel.
  • Two regimes, and the transition is the result: at low wheel speed the disc sticks, is carried up, then slips back — a relaxation / stick-slip oscillation. At high speed the friction force becomes roughly constant (kinetic) and the disc settles at a static deflection angle where tangential friction balances the gravity torque.
  • Expect a critical wheel speed separating oscillation from steady deflection — that is the plot to build the talk around.
  • Negative damping from velocity-weakening friction (the negative slope of the µ–v curve) gives self-excited oscillation growing into a limit cycle. Same mechanism as a bowed string or a squealing brake.
  • Interesting physics — but every observable is mediated by a quantity you cannot reproduce.

Attacks

  1. "Reproduce yesterday's friction coefficient." It varies with humidity, temperature, wear and contamination, and it drifts during your own run.
  2. "There are two contacts, not one." The undriven wheel is a second friction source your model omits entirely.
  3. "The wheel wore during the experiment." The contact changes as you run.
  4. "How did you set and hold the normal force?" It is the most important parameter and it is uncontrolled.
  5. "Your motor speed droops under load." You set a voltage, not a speed.
  6. "Disc runout injects periodic forcing at the rotation frequency." You may have measured eccentricity, not the phenomenon.
  7. "You cannot tell sticking from slipping." Without instrumentation you are guessing at the mechanism.
  8. "Why did you choose this?" Even a successful investigation has no visual or narrative payoff.
Worst attack: #1. Friction is the mechanism here, so irreproducible friction is not a correction to your result — it is your result being noise.
Countermeasures
  • There is no good defence. If forced onto this problem: instrument the normal force with a load cell, close the loop on motor speed, replace the wheel surface on a schedule, log humidity, and report everything as distributions over many trials rather than single curves.
  • Best available move: reframe around the regime transition (oscillation → static deflection), because a qualitative transition is more robust than any quantitative friction value.
